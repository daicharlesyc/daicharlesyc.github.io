Recently, I've been reading two very interesting books, called "The Underlying Logic: How to See the Essence of Things" by Liu Run (which is in Chinese) and "Poor Charlie's Almanack", a compilation of talks by Charlie Munger, the famous associate of Warren Buffett. While reading them, it struck me that they had a common approach to thinking, which Charlie Munger calls "mental models", and I used to call "points of view" in my head. I shared my thoughts about mental models in an English course last Friday, but I still have so many things to say about it, so I decided to turn it into a blog post.

## A little about myself

It is always interesting to see your deepest intuitions presented under another name in a book. Over the past three years, I have been collecting different "points of view", because I had realized that my way of thinking was very narrow and limited. Before I entered preparatory classes (a special college system in France, preparing students for entrance exams of "Higher Schools"), I think that I had been building up my identity around maths and informatics olympiads, and relied too much on them. When I had difficulties in humanities (because I didn't spend time studying them), I often dismissed them as useless, and comforted myself with my achievements in olympiads. This might have done little harm if I hadn't repeated this pattern across many other areas of my life. But then I entered preparatory classes, and since I was accepted at a boarding school, I began to spend much, much more time with my peers. And it made me realize that I had to change and broaden my perspective, because I had real gaps in general knowledge and an incomplete worldview, accompanied by some lack of empathy. Also, there are humanities in the entrance exams for Higher Schools, which was also a motivation for studying them. 

Thus, at first, motivated by my friends, I began to read philosophy books. My first one was Thus Spoke Zarathustra (it was hard to understand, so I was also reading an analysis in parallel), which was a completely novel kind of point of view for me. From then on, I began to collect "points of view": my mind was hungry for spirituality, and I read taoist books, which I really loved, biographies of the Buddha and Muhammad, the Bhagavad-Gita (it was also short enough so I read it four times in a single week), and practiced journaling and meditation every day for months (the Youtuber Dr. K was a great guide, and I am really grateful to him). I gained a very deep understanding of myself during this period, greatly increased my awareness and self-control skills (I was really addicted to mobile games and webnovels, but now I don't have problems with these anymore), and also built an identity centered around things other than scientific skills. Or, to be more accurate, these readings encouraged me to view my identity, the "I", as something very malleable and always changing, and to realize that I shouldn't define myself solely by my strengths, as I did before by telling myself that humanities were either useless or that I wasn't made for them. My grades in humanities also improved drastically, proving my past self wrong: I went from 4/20 at my first French mock exam to 16/20 at the real entrance exam. And my friends also told me that they felt that I was slowly becoming more empathic (I was practicing meditation specifically for this).

Thus, it felt particularly meaningful for me to read about these "mental models" and have a deep resonance with the "points of view" I had been collecting all along the way. Maybe I should now talk a little bit about what I have read. But I'm going to share it with my own understanding, because you can read the books if you just want the original ideas.

## Some useful mental models ?

More than 2500 years ago, the Buddha shared us his teaching. I think that the two most important parts are the worldview he taught ([the Four Noble Truths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths)), and a method to reach enlightenment ([the Noble Eightfold Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path)). The Noble Eightfold Path consists of 8 principles to follow in order to reach enlightenment: having the right view, right thoughts, right speech, right conduct, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration. What is interesting here, is that having the right view is listed first. And put like this, it seems trivial: of course, you need to have the correct views in order to succeed in any enterprise ! But in real life, how many of us have taken the time to question thoroughly our deepest assumptions ? Who has tried to <i>philosophize with a hammer</i>, and directed the hammer against himself ? 

Charlie Munger makes the choice of mental models explicit. He tells us that, in order to succeed in life, you need to have the correct mental models. He talks about success in business and investing, but, as our example with the Buddha shows, it is true for many areas of life. Charlie adds: "To the man with only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail" (which is kind of ironic to quote after I talked about <i>philosophizing with a hammer</i>). This is why it is useful to have a diversity of mental models. But which mental models does he propose ? He begins with some of the most reliable mental models that modern society has developed: mathematics, hard science, engineering. As concrete examples, he cites basic arithmetic, elementary probabilities, decision tree theory, Gaussian distribution, backup systems, critical mass, breakpoints... (I shall give an example of personal mental models I have been thinking of in mathematics and physics, showing how you can relate a seemingly abstract statement to your life). Accounting is very important if you want to invest seriously or start a business. And of course, there are less precise, but equally important mental models about human nature: physiology, psychology, microeconomics. I won't go into details here, because the point of this blog is about sharing my thoughts (and I don't have the knowledge to write a blog about physiology xD).

## Two beautiful ideas

Anyway, I wanted to present two relatively simple ideas that I found very beautiful. The first one is the idea of <i>phase transition</i>, which comes from physics. The idea is that a physical system (think of this abstractly: anything can be a physical system), has macroscopic states called phases (for example, water can be liquid, solid or gas), and underlying physical quantities (for water, it is pressure, volume, and temperature). And as the quantities evolve continuously, sometimes, the physical system encounters <i>phase transitions</i>: it has a global reorganization which changes the macroscopic properties (if you heat water, it will suddenly begin to boil at some point). And I like to think of this, because our brain is an example of physical system, and it feels like we have some kind of sudden transitions when we learn or grow. For example, when we learn something, we all have the feeling of progressing rapidly during short periods of time, then being stuck for a long period, then again having a rapid progression, and being stuck etc.. And I like to imagine that our brains are also physical systems having some kind of phase transitions (I wonder if this is because the brain has to continuously find more efficient ways to organize the information and decision process to allow further progression, but this is something that an analogy can't tell). 

This helps me a lot to avoid being frustrated when I feel stuck, because I have already experienced very short periods during which I made tremendous progress in an area after long plateaus. We often expect the learning curve to be continuously increasing or even to progress at a constant rate, but the reality is often harsh. This mental model reminds me to evaluate rationally if it is worth continuing on when I feel discouraged, rather than following my feelings blindly.

The second idea I want to present is more abstract, but very beautiful. It comes from a very profound and beautiful mathematical language, called category theory. As its name indicates, it is about categories, which are collections of objects and arrows between them (called morphisms, think of them as a special kind of mathematical relationship).

![Image of a diagram]({{ '/assets/image/category.jpg' | relative_url }})

*An example of diagram in category theory. A, B, C are the objects, 1\_A, 1\_B, 1\_C, f, g, fg are names for arrows.*

In this theory, there is a very interesting mathematical property, called Yoneda's lemma. Formally, a consequence of Yoneda’s lemma is that an object is determined up to isomorphism by the morphisms into it from all objects in the category, together with how those morphisms compose. More roughly, the structure of the relationships of an object with the others in a category <i>determines</i> the object. And it is easy to see that the idea of looking at relationships instead of the objects is very deep, and goes way beyond the field of mathematics, because it reminds us that we can understand an object or an idea by understanding its relationships with other objects. For example, we can learn about someone by observing his behavior, or, as my English teacher quoted André Malraux, "We learn more about a Greek statue by comparing it with an Egyptian statue than by studying ten Greek statues".

I also thought about this when watching a video about Heidegger. Roughly, it seems that Heidegger tells us that objects in our surroundings aren't only seen as objects: we first think of them as <i>tools (Zeug)</i>. Sometimes, they are <i>ready-to-hand</i> and we are so used to them that we don't even think about them, but sometimes, they are <i>present-at-hand</i>, when we stop using it, and start inspecting it as a standalone object. As I heard this, it made me think that what Heidegger was talking about, was not only about objects, but about <i>the relationship between humans and objects</i>. And as a relationship, it has both implications for the objects (some are used, some are inspected), and for humans (the two modes of existence of objects described correspond in fact to two predominant modes of interaction: we like to think of objects as either tools or objects of study, which tells a lot about us). My point here is not what I have been thinking about Heidegger (although Heidegger is interesting). It is what this mental model has unlocked: viewing the <i>relationship</i> as an object of thought in its own right.

I've applied this mental model to Heidegger, but you can apply it to practically anything, as anything has relationships with other things and can be compared! I think this mental model is very interesting because it encourages us to think of things as a system with interactions rather than isolated objects. And as in the comparison between Greek and Egyptian statues, we shouldn't only focus on analogies, but also on the differences that arise during comparisons.

Speaking of analogy, I also used these two mental models as analogies. We should be aware not only of where they work, but also of their limitations. Never forget to *philosophize with a hammer* and test your assumptions! This is another reason why I value having diverse mental models: when I relied on only a few, I tended to tie my identity to them, which made them harder to question.
